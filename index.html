      Technical knowledge to have

      The failures and standards that come up at review, then places to ask questions, find the code, read guidance and run a quick check. Where something lines up with an attribute, we've said which.

      Failures that changed practice

      Reviewers often ask you to describe an industry failure and what changed because of it. Pick one close to your own work, and know what happened, why it happened, and what is done differently now. Tap one to open it.

      Toddbrook Reservoir, Whaley Bridge 2019 · dam spillway

      What happened: in heavy rain on 1 August 2019, part of the concrete spillway on a 19th-century dam broke up. About 1,500 people were evacuated while the dam was shored up and the reservoir drawn down.

      What changed: an independent review led by Prof David Balmforth made 22 recommendations, all accepted by government. A wider review of reservoir safety and a reform programme followed, moving towards a more risk-based regime.

      The review on GOV.UK · Attribute 4 · Attribute 1

      Grenfell Tower, London 2017 · fire and cladding

      What happened: 72 people died when fire spread up the outside of a 24-storey residential block that had been refurbished with combustible cladding. The Inquiry's final report (4 September 2024) found failures by government, regulators, manufacturers, designers and contractors over many years.

      What changed: Dame Judith Hackitt's review, Building a Safer Future (May 2018), led to the Building Safety Act 2022, the Building Safety Regulator and new competence duties (see below).

      Phase 2 report on GOV.UK · Attribute 4 · Attribute 7

      Carillion 2018 · contractor insolvency

      What happened: one of the UK's largest contractors declared insolvency on 15 January 2018, part-way through hospitals, roads and rail work. MPs blamed aggressive accounting, bidding work at too little margin, and paying suppliers late.

      What changed: more scrutiny of contractors' finances and of bidding on lowest price, and more attention to prompt payment down the supply chain. A good example for commercial questions.

      The MPs' inquiry · Attribute 3 · Attribute 2

      Ronan Point, east London 1968 · progressive collapse

      What happened: a gas explosion in a flat blew out a load-bearing wall panel in a 22-storey precast block, and a whole corner of the building collapsed floor by floor. Four people died.

      What changed: designing against disproportionate collapse (robustness and tying) entered the Building Regulations. It's now requirement A3 in Approved Document A.

      National Archives · Attribute 1

      Nicoll Highway, Singapore 2004 · deep excavation

      What happened: a braced excavation for a metro line collapsed, killing four. The inquiry found errors in detailing the bracing connections and a design that overestimated the soil's strength.

      What changed: a standard case for checking temporary works design independently and for acting on monitoring readings before they reach trigger levels.

      MIT summary · Attribute 1 · Attribute 4

      Hyatt Regency walkways, Kansas City 1981 · connection design

      What happened: two suspended walkways fell into a crowded atrium, and 113 people died. The hanger connections had too little capacity: the detail was changed during fabrication and the change wasn't properly checked.

      What changed: the reference case for who owns a design change, and for checking connections, not just members.

      NIST investigation · Attribute 1 · Attribute 7

      Morandi bridge, Genoa 2018 · ageing asset

      What happened: a span of a 1960s motorway viaduct collapsed, killing 43. Its stays were steel encased in concrete, hard to inspect, and later inquiries pointed to poor maintenance and design weaknesses.

      What changed: renewed attention, in Italy and beyond, to inspecting ageing structures whose critical parts can't be seen.

      Designing Buildings summary · Attribute 1 · Attribute 2

      CROSS-UK safety reports ongoing · near misses

      What it is: a confidential scheme where engineers report structural and fire safety concerns. Reports are published anonymously with expert comment. The Building Safety Regulator named it the official voluntary reporting system for building safety until at least 2028.

      Why read it: the reports are short, recent and close to everyday work, so they're easy to use as examples at review.

      How the references fit together

      From law to good practice

      Technical references sit in a rough order of authority. The top two tiers are law. Everything below is not law in itself, but it becomes binding when a regulation, a contract, a client or an adopting body calls it up, and it is the yardstick your work is judged against if something goes wrong.

      More legal force
      Law
      Acts of ParliamentPrimary legislation. Sets duties and powers in broad terms.
      Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 · Building Safety Act 2022 · Reservoirs Act 1975
      Law
      RegulationsSecondary legislation made under an Act. The detailed legal requirements.
      CDM 2015 · Building Regulations 2010
      Route to compliance
      Approved Documents and Approved Codes of PracticeOfficial guidance on meeting the regulations. Not compulsory, but following it is normally taken to show compliance; depart from it and you must show your way is as good.
      Approved Documents A to T · HSE ACoPs and guidance
      Binding when called up
      British, European and international standardsVoluntary in themselves. Made mandatory by a regulation, a specification or a contract, and cited as recognised practice.
      Eurocodes with UK National Annexes · BS 5975 · other BS, BS EN and ISO standards
      Binding when called up
      Sector, client and adoption standardsRules set by an owner or adopting body for its own assets. Mandatory on its network or for adoption; departures need its approval.
      DMRB · Sewerage Sector Guidance · council highway design guides · infrastructure owners' standards
      Good practice
      Industry guidanceResearch-based guidance from industry bodies. Not mandatory, but shows what a competent engineer would be expected to know, and planning policy or a contract often calls it up.
      CIRIA guides, including the SuDS Manual · Manual for Streets · institution and trade body guidance

      A general picture, not legal advice. Status varies by jurisdiction (England, Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland each have their own regulations and guidance) and by contract: a specification or contract can make any document in the lower tiers binding on that project.

      Standards and law to know

      What each one governs and when it applies to you. You won't need them all, but you should know the ones on your projects well.

      Eurocodes and UK National Annexes structural design

      The European design codes (EN 1990 to 1999) cover the basis of design, loads and each material. The UK National Annex to each one sets the UK's values, so you always use the two together. Second-generation Eurocodes are being published up to 2027, and BSI says the UK versions of the current ones will be withdrawn on 30 March 2028. Know which generation your project uses.

      BSI on the next generation · Attribute 1

      CDM 2015 all construction work

      Applies to every construction project. As a designer you must eliminate, reduce or control foreseeable risks to people who build, use and maintain what you design, and pass on what others need to know. Projects with more than one contractor need a principal designer and a principal contractor. Expect a question on it at review.

      HSE guidance (L153, free) · Attribute 4

      Building Safety Act 2022 buildings, especially higher-risk

      Higher-risk buildings (at least 18 m or seven storeys, mainly residential) go through the Building Safety Regulator at gateways: approval before building work starts (Gateway 2) and before occupation (Gateway 3). All building work now has dutyholders with competence duties, including designers.

      GOV.UK guidance · Attribute 4 · Attribute 7

      Reservoirs Act 1975 dams and reservoirs

      In England it covers reservoirs holding 25,000 m³ or more above the surrounding land. High-risk reservoirs must always have a supervising engineer and be inspected at least every 10 years by an inspecting engineer, both from government panels of qualified civil engineers.

      Environment Agency guidance · Attribute 4

      DMRB motorways and trunk roads

      The Design Manual for Roads and Bridges sets the standards for the strategic road network. Doing something different needs an approved departure from standard. Many local authorities use it too.

      Standards for Highways · Attribute 1

      Manual for Streets 1 and 2 local streets

      Department for Transport guidance (2007) for residential streets, putting place before movement. Manual for Streets 2 extends it to busier streets such as high streets and town centres. Used instead of DMRB for most local roads.

      GOV.UK · Attribute 5

      BS 5975 temporary works

      The code of practice for temporary works, now in two parts (2024): Part 1 covers managing temporary works, including the temporary works coordinator and categories of design check; Part 2 covers falsework design. If you work for or with a contractor, know who your temporary works coordinator is.

      BSI announcement · Attribute 4

      Local and sector standards

      Much day-to-day work isn't governed by national codes. Drainage that a water company will adopt, and roads that a council will adopt, have to meet their own guidance. Checked 22 Nov 2026.

      Sewerage Sector Guidance sewers for adoption, England

      The Ofwat-approved rules, in force since April 2020, for building sewers that a water company will adopt. Its Appendix C, the Design and Construction Guidance, replaced Sewers for Adoption and sets pipe sizes, gradients, manholes, pumping stations and SuDS features that can be adopted. Free.

      Water UK's page still calls version 2.2 (July 2022) the latest, but it also hosts version 2.3, approved in November 2023. Check with your water company which one it's applying. Each company also has its own developer services pages and local requirements.

      Water UK: codes for adoption · Attribute 1 · Attribute 5

      Approved Document H building drainage, England

      The Building Regulations guidance for foul and surface water drainage from buildings, up to the point where it joins a public sewer, plus septic tanks and soakaways. The current edition is 2015. Free.

      GOV.UK · Attribute 1

      Wales and Scotland sewer adoption outside England

      In Wales, see the Welsh Government's guidance on mandatory adoption of sewers and lateral drains (2012). In Scotland, Scottish Water's Sewers for Scotland, 4th edition applies to designs submitted from 1 January 2019. Both are free.

      Attribute 1

      Council highway design guides and standard details roads for adoption

      When a developer builds a road for the council to adopt (a section 38 agreement), or works on an existing road (section 278), the council's own design guide, specification and standard detail drawings govern. DMRB is written for motorways and trunk roads; councils mostly lean on Manual for Streets for layout and cite DMRB only for particular things, such as road safety audits. Each council's rules differ, so search for "[your council] highway design guide" or "section 38".

      Some examples, all free:

      • Essex Design Guide: Highways Technical Manual: street types, junctions, visibility, lighting and adoption, with links to Essex's standard construction drawings.
      • Suffolk: design manual, specification and standard drawings: drainage, pavements, kerbs and footways, plus commuted sum rates for 2026–27.
      • Leicestershire Highway Design Guide: technical approval and section 38: the approval and adoption process step by step.
      • Kirklees: highways adoption guidance and standard details: about 20 guidance notes, for developments in Kirklees only.

        Our advice: online

        • Do a test Teams call a few days before, from the same room and computer. Practise sharing your slides and uploading a file.
        • Have a backup connection ready, such as a phone hotspot, and keep your laptop plugged in.
        • If you drop out, rejoin straight away from the invitation link. Keep any contact details ICE gives you to hand in case you can't.
        • Keep a PDF of your slides open in case your slide software won't share.
        • Turn off notifications and close other apps. Put the camera at eye level with light in front of you.

        Our advice: in person

        • Plan your journey to arrive well before the 30-minute registration time, and allow for delays.
        • Fully charge your laptop, since no power supply is provided. Bring printed A3 copies of key slides as a backup.
        • If your letter doesn't make clear how you'll upload the task at the venue, ask ICE beforehand.

        The interview

        ICE
        • Two peer reviewers assess you; a third may join for specialist knowledge, and an observer may sit in without taking part. pp. 4, 12-13
        • The presentation and interview together last up to 75 minutes for a Chartered review60 minutes for an Incorporated review. p. 13
        • Reviewers confirm you've reached the required level in all the attributes. Where your report is light on an attribute, expect specific questions on it. p. 13
        • Phones off before the interview starts; recording isn't allowed. p. 13

        Our advice

        • Answer in the first person and give a real example for each claim: the situation, what you did, and the result.
        • Know every number in your report. If one is wrong, correct it before they find it.
        • If you don't know, say so. Then say what you do know, how you'd find out, and how you'd keep things safe meanwhile. Never bluff.
        • Expect four kinds of question: factual, analytical (compare, explain why), hypothetical (what would you do if?) and probing (why did you decide that?). ICE blog
        • It's fine to pause, ask them to repeat a question, or sketch to explain. Practise two or three sketches of your own project.
        • Do at least one mock review, ideally with someone who isn't your supervisor.
        • Revise the Code of Conduct, CPD, H&S and current affairs: the attributes cover them all.

        ICE

        Our advice

        • Split the 90 minutes: about 10 to choose and plan, 65 to write, 15 to check. Planning is what makes it read clearly.
        • Split the 60 minutes: about 10 to choose and plan, 40 to write, 10 to check. Planning is what makes it read clearly.
        • Write for the audience and style you're given. A blog post for the public reads differently from a note to a client.
        • Lay it out like a real technical note: a clear title, who it's for, the purpose, the facts, and your conclusion or recommendation.
        • Use a simple structure: the issue, why it matters, the options or arguments, your view, and what should happen next.
        • Bring in a current example and, where it fits, sustainability, safety and cost.
        • Fill your two A4 sides with facts and figures on likely topics (from the News page), not pre-written paragraphs.

            The seven attributes

            1. Engineering: sound theory and judgement on real problems; options, risk, whole life.
            2. Leadership: plan, organise, monitor; develop people; improve quality.
            3. Commercial: procurement, your contract, budgets, programme, change and claims.
            4. H&S: HSWA, CDM 2015, duties, hierarchy of control, promoting good practice.
            5. Sustainability: carbon (PAS 2080), BNG, social value, adaptation.
            6. Communication: different audiences, non-technical explanation, disagreement, inclusion.
            7. Professional: the Code, CPD, judgement within your limits, public interest.

            CEng: develops solutions to engineering problems using new or existing technologies, through innovation, creativity and change.IEng: maintains and manages applications of current and developing technology.

              Review day / Presentation tips

              Your 15 minutes, built on your report

              The presentation takes one aspect of a project you've already written about and goes deeper. Here's how to choose it, structure it, and design slides that help rather than get in the way.

              What ICE says
              ICE
              • 15 minutes, the same for IEng and CEng. Reviewers may stop you if you go over. p. 13
              • It should expand on one aspect of your report. It should not cover all the attributes, or summarise your CV or report. p. 13
              • Visual aids are encouraged. Online you share your screen on Teams; in person, visual aids can be up to A3 and you can bring a laptop, but no power supply is provided. pp. 13, 26

              ICE doesn't set a number of slides or a structure. Everything else on this page is our advice.

              The golden rule

              Go deeper into a project that's already in your report

              Our advice

              • Use a project from your report. Your reviewers have read it and will question you from it. A new project means claims they can't check against your written evidence, and time spent setting the scene.
              • Go deeper, don't repeat. Pick one aspect, such as a decision, a problem you solved or an improvement you led, and add what the report had no room for: the options, the numbers, the drawings, the trade-offs.
              • Make it yours. The reviewers are assessing you, not the project. Every slide should show something you did, decided or changed.
              • Keep it consistent. Use the same facts and figures as your report. If something in the report was wrong, say so and correct it.
              Choosing the aspect

              A good topic passes these tests

              • 1It's in your report. You can point to the section where you wrote about it.
              • 2You made the call. You led the decision or the work, not just helped with it.
              • 3There was a real choice. More than one option, with a trade-off you can explain.
              • 4It has numbers. Cost, carbon, programme, risk or performance figures you can talk to.
              • 5It can be shown. Drawings, sections, photos or a simple chart, not just words.
              • 6It fits in 13 minutes. One story, told well, beats three told in a rush.

              Several attributes will come through naturally (engineering judgement, commercial, safety, communication). That's fine, but don't try to tour all seven: ICE says not to.

              Structure and timing

              About 13 minutes and 8 to 12 slides

              Our advice

              Our advice: slides

              • Roughly one slide per minute to a minute and a half: 8 to 12 slides in total. Fewer is fine; more than 15 usually means you're rushing.
              • One idea per slide, with a headline that says the point ("Option C saved 6 weeks"), not a label ("Options").
              • Mostly pictures: drawings, sections, site photos, a simple chart. Keep text to a few words you can talk around.
              • Put the key number on the slide, large. Reviewers remember numbers.
              • Keep backup slides after your summary for likely questions (calculations, a programme, a risk register extract). Don't present them unless asked.

              Our advice: delivery

              • Say "I" for what you did, and "we" only for the team. Be clear which is which.
              • Talk to the reviewers, not the slides. Notes are fine; reading a script isn't.
              • Spend the most time on your decision and why. That's where the attributes show.
              • Rehearse aloud with a timer at least five times, then once with someone who'll interrupt you.
              • Check what you can show: remove anything confidential, or get your employer's and client's agreement.

              Common mistakes

              What trips candidates up

              • A new topic. Presenting work that isn't in your report, so reviewers can't link it to your written evidence.
              • The project tour. Describing the scheme (location, client, team, programme) instead of what you did and decided.
              • All seven attributes. Ticking off every attribute in turn. ICE says not to.
              • Too many slides. 20 or more slides in 15 minutes means rushing and skipping.
              • Reading the slides. If the slide says it all, the reviewers don't need you.
              • "We" all the way through. Reviewers can't tell what was yours.
              • Running over. Reviewers may stop you at 15 minutes, before your result and lessons.
              • Numbers you can't explain. Every figure on a slide is a likely question.
              Into the questions

              Finish cleanly and hand over

              Our advice

              • End on a summary slide with your decision, the result and one lesson, and leave it up. It gives the reviewers a starting point.
              • Close clearly: "That's the end of my presentation. I'm happy to take questions." Then stop talking.
              • Expect the first questions to come from your presentation. Prepare answers to the five questions in your plan, with backup slides where a picture helps.
              • If a question goes beyond what you did, say so, then say how you'd approach it. Don't bluff.

                    Tools / Manhole detail

                    Manhole detail generator

                    Enter the cover level, the pipes and the chamber, and the section, plan and calculation sheet redraw as you type. It flags clashes and anything outside the limits you set, and exports the detail and the calcs.

                    What this is, and what it isn't tap to read the assumptions
                    • Everything is drawn and calculated from first principles using your inputs. It doesn't reproduce any standard and doesn't check compliance with one.
                    • Inputs marked default are placeholders. Set them from the governing documents for your job (for example the sewer adoption or highway specification) and the supplier's data.
                    • Pipe outside diameters are estimated from the bore with a rough ratio for each material unless you enter the manufacturer's figure.
                    • Ground is taken as cover level, the excavation as a rectangular pit, and the chamber walls as seated at the outlet invert. The channel runs straight through on the centreline, with a half-round invert and vertical sides up to the benching.
                    • Rest platforms, backdrops, pipe joints near the chamber and reinforcement aren't drawn. The checks tell you when they're needed by your own limits.
                    • Your inputs are saved in this browser only.
